## The testing sequence

Serum thyroid-stimulating hormone is the initial test in suspected thyroid dysfunction, because it
responds to small changes in thyroid hormone before the hormone levels themselves leave the
reference range — the relationship is logarithmic, so a modest fall in thyroxine produces a large
rise in the stimulating hormone. Where the result is raised, free thyroxine is measured to
distinguish two situations:

- **Overt hypothyroidism** — raised thyroid-stimulating hormone with free thyroxine below the
  reference range.
- **Subclinical hypothyroidism** — raised thyroid-stimulating hormone with free thyroxine within
  the reference range.

Thyroid peroxidase antibodies establish autoimmune thyroiditis as the cause and help estimate how
likely subclinical disease is to progress. Routine thyroid ultrasound is not part of the workup for
an abnormal blood test in the absence of a palpable nodule or goiter.

## Confirming before treating

An abnormal result is confirmed with a repeat measurement after several weeks before long-term
therapy starts. The hormone fluctuates, is suppressed during acute illness and recovery from it, and
shows diurnal variation with higher values overnight and early morning. Treating a single abnormal
value is a common and avoidable error, and non-thyroidal illness is the usual explanation for an
abnormal panel in someone who is acutely unwell.

## Monitoring interval

This is the interval most often asked about and most often got wrong. Thyroid-stimulating hormone is
rechecked six to eight weeks after starting levothyroxine or after any dose change, because it takes
that long to re-equilibrate to the new steady state. A test taken at two or three weeks still
largely reflects the old state and invites an unnecessary further change, producing an oscillation
that takes months to settle. Once the level is stable on an unchanged dose, testing moves to six to
twelve month intervals.

## Absorption and interference

Levothyroxine is taken consistently with respect to food, and separated from calcium salts, iron
salts, proton pump inhibitors and several other agents that reduce its absorption. Where a dose that
was previously adequate stops working, adherence and interfering agents are reviewed before the dose
is increased — a newly started supplement is a more common explanation than a change in the thyroid
itself.

## Where guidance differs

Whether to treat subclinical hypothyroidism is a genuine and unresolved disagreement. There is broad
agreement on treating when thyroid-stimulating hormone is persistently above about 10 mIU/L. In the
band between the upper reference limit and 10 mIU/L, some guidance describes a trial of treatment
where symptoms, positive antibodies, or cardiovascular risk are present, while other guidance
describes monitoring alone for most people, citing trials in older adults that showed no
symptomatic benefit from treatment. Bodies also differ on whether the reference range should be
adjusted upward for older adults, in whom a mildly raised value may be normal for age. Pregnancy is
treated separately under trimester-specific ranges, with a lower threshold for treatment.
