## What it is

Migraine is a recurrent neurological disorder whose most prominent feature is headache, but which
also involves sensory sensitivity, nausea and, in some people, transient neurological symptoms. It
is episodic in most people and chronic in a minority, where chronic migraine is defined as headache
on fifteen or more days per month, of which at least eight have migraine features, for more than
three months.

## The typical attack

A migraine headache is classically one-sided, pulsating, moderate to severe, and made worse by
routine physical activity; it is accompanied by nausea, vomiting, or sensitivity to light and sound.
Untreated attacks commonly last from several hours to three days. Not every attack has every
feature, and the headache is bilateral in a substantial proportion of people — which is one reason
migraine is frequently mislabeled as sinus or tension headache.

Attacks often have phases: a premonitory phase hours to a day beforehand with mood change, yawning,
food cravings or neck stiffness; in about a third of people an aura; the headache itself; and a
recovery phase with fatigue and difficulty concentrating. Recognizing the premonitory phase is
practically useful, because it is the point at which acute treatment works best.

## Aura

Aura is a set of fully reversible neurological symptoms that usually develop over five or more
minutes and last less than an hour. Most often visual — flickering lights, zigzag lines, or an area
of lost vision that expands across the visual field — and less often sensory, with spreading numbness
or tingling, or affecting speech. Aura most often precedes the headache but can accompany it or
occur without any headache at all, which is disconcerting the first time it happens.

Aura that begins abruptly at full intensity, that lasts longer than an hour, or that involves
weakness rather than sensory change is not typical and prompts assessment for another cause.

## Common triggers

Irregular sleep, missed meals, dehydration, stress and the let-down period after stress, hormonal
change around menstruation, alcohol, certain foods in susceptible individuals, weather change, bright
or flickering light, and overuse of acute headache medication. Triggers are individual and often
additive rather than absolute — a food that is tolerated when rested may not be after a poor night.
A headache diary is the usual way to identify them, since recall alone reliably produces false
associations by noticing the memorable and ignoring the times nothing happened.

## How it is generally managed

Three parts: treating attacks early when they occur, reducing frequency with preventive treatment
when attacks are frequent or disabling, and addressing contributing factors including sleep, meal
regularity, and overuse of acute medication. Treatment thresholds, drug classes and
medication-overuse limits are covered by the migraine guideline note in this corpus.

## When urgent assessment is advised

A headache is not a migraine question when it is sudden and reaches maximum intensity within about a
minute, when it comes with fever and a stiff neck, when it follows a head injury, when it comes with
new weakness, numbness, confusion, visual loss or difficulty speaking that does not resolve, when it
begins after age 50 as a new headache type, or when it worsens progressively over days or weeks.
These require urgent medical assessment. The emergency-symptom notes in this corpus govern them.
