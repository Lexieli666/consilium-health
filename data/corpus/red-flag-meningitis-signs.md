> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## Call emergency services immediately

- Fever with a severe headache and a stiff neck.
- A rash of small red or purple spots or bruises **that does not fade when pressed** — tested by
  rolling a clear glass over it.
- Fever with confusion, drowsiness, difficulty waking, or seizure.
- Severe dislike of bright light, alongside headache and fever.
- Cold hands and feet with a fever, mottled skin, and severe limb or joint pain.
- Rapid deterioration in someone who seemed to have an ordinary febrile illness.

In babies and young children: a high-pitched or moaning cry, irritability when handled, refusing
feeds, a bulging soft spot on the head, floppiness or stiffness, unusual drowsiness, and pale,
blotchy or mottled skin.

## Why the classic triad is not the trigger

Fever, neck stiffness and altered consciousness together are the textbook presentation, and all
three are present in only a minority of cases. Waiting for the full triad delays treatment in the
majority. The trigger for escalation is any suggestive combination, particularly in someone
deteriorating over hours.

## The rash appears late, and its absence proves nothing

The non-blanching rash of meningococcal septicemia is a well-known sign, and it is a **late** one.
Many people are seriously unwell before any rash appears, and some never develop one. Escalation on
the earlier features — fever with severe headache, cold extremities with fever, limb pain, rapid
deterioration — is what changes outcomes. Where a non-blanching rash is present, the situation is
already an emergency and every minute counts.

Cold hands and feet, severe leg pain, and abnormal skin color are recognized early features of
meningococcal disease in adolescents and young adults, frequently preceding the classic signs by
hours, and are commonly attributed to something ordinary at the time.

## Why the urgency is absolute

Bacterial meningitis and meningococcal septicemia can progress from mild symptoms to
life-threatening illness within hours, and survival and the risk of permanent harm — deafness,
limb loss, brain injury — depend directly on how quickly treatment starts.

## What not to do

Do not wait to see whether a fever settles when these features are present. Do not be reassured that
the person has been seen earlier the same day and sent home; rapid deterioration after an initial
assessment is characteristic of this illness, and returning is appropriate rather than excessive.

## Contacts

Close household and intimate contacts of a confirmed meningococcal case are usually offered
preventive antibiotics, arranged by public health services. Anyone in that position contacts health
services rather than waiting for symptoms.
