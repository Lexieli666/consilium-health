## What it is

Anemia (anaemia in British spelling) is a reduced concentration of hemoglobin (haemoglobin), the
oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells. Iron deficiency is its most common cause worldwide. Iron
is required to make hemoglobin, so when body iron stores are exhausted, red cells are produced
smaller and paler than normal — the microcytic, hypochromic pattern seen on a blood count.

The critical point about iron-deficiency anemia in adults is that it is a **finding, not a
diagnosis**. Iron is not lost from the body in significant amounts except through bleeding, so
deficiency in an adult who is not menstruating heavily or pregnant raises the question of where the
iron went. Treating the anemia without answering that question can leave a gastrointestinal cancer
undetected, which is the reason the workup exists.

## How it is usually recognized

Fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, breathlessness on exertion, palpitations, headache, dizziness,
pale skin, cold hands and feet, brittle nails, and hair thinning. Less familiar features that are
relatively specific to iron deficiency include restless legs and pica — craving non-food substances,
classically ice.

Onset is usually gradual, and the body compensates well, so people frequently attribute months of
increasing fatigue to their circumstances and present only when compensation fails. Anemia is often
found incidentally on a blood count taken for another reason.

## Establishing the cause

The blood count shows the pattern; ferritin is the primary test of iron stores, with the caveat that
it rises in inflammation and can therefore appear normal despite deficiency, in which case
transferrin saturation and other iron studies clarify it. Once deficiency is confirmed, the source
is investigated: menstrual and pregnancy history, dietary intake, medications including
anti-inflammatories and anticoagulants, celiac serology, and — where no adequate explanation exists,
particularly in men and post-menopausal women — investigation of the gastrointestinal tract.

## What contributes to it

Blood loss: heavy menstrual bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding from ulcers, inflammatory disease,
polyps or cancer, and frequent blood donation. Increased requirement: pregnancy, breastfeeding,
infancy and adolescence. Reduced absorption: celiac disease, previous gastric surgery, *Helicobacter
pylori* infection, and long-term acid suppression. Inadequate intake, which is a sufficient
explanation more often in restricted diets and in children than in adults.

## How it is generally managed

Correcting the underlying cause, together with iron replacement — oral in most cases, intravenous
where oral is not tolerated, not absorbed, or where correction needs to be rapid. Replacement
continues after the hemoglobin normalizes in order to refill stores, which is the step most often
stopped too early, and the response is confirmed by repeat testing rather than assumed. Doses are
outside the scope of this system.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Chest pain, breathlessness at rest, fainting, a rapid heart rate at rest, or vomiting blood or
passing black tarry stools require urgent assessment. The emergency-symptom notes in this corpus
govern these situations.
