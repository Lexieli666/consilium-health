## Annual vaccination

United States guidance describes routine annual influenza vaccination for everyone aged 6 months and
older who has no contraindication, rather than restricting it to high-risk groups. Vaccination
before the end of October is described as the usual aim in the northern hemisphere, early enough to
precede typical circulation and not so early that protection wanes before the season ends. Later
vaccination remains worthwhile while the virus is circulating.

Annual repetition is necessary for two reasons that are often conflated: the circulating strains
change, so the vaccine composition is reformulated each year, and protection from a given dose wanes
over months regardless.

Higher-dose or adjuvanted formulations are preferentially described for adults aged 65 and over, in
whom the standard-dose response is weaker. Egg-free formulations exist, and current guidance
describes egg allergy of any severity as no longer requiring special precautions beyond those for
any vaccine.

## Groups where the benefit is largest

Adults 65 and over, children under 5 and especially under 2, pregnancy and the postpartum period,
residents of long-term care, people with chronic lung, heart, kidney, liver, neurologic or metabolic
disease, immunosuppression, and people with a body mass index in the obese range. Vaccination in
pregnancy also confers protection on the infant in the first months of life, before the infant can
be vaccinated directly. Health-care workers are included on transmission grounds as well as personal
ones.

## Antiviral treatment and its timing

Antiviral treatment is described as most effective when started within 48 hours of symptom onset,
and the benefit falls as that window closes. Treatment is recommended as early as possible, without
waiting for laboratory confirmation, for:

- Anyone hospitalized with suspected or confirmed influenza.
- Anyone with severe, complicated or progressive illness.
- Anyone at higher risk of complications, per the groups above.

For otherwise healthy outpatients presenting within 48 hours, treatment is described as a
reasonable option based on shared decision-making, with a modest reduction in symptom duration.
Treatment started after 48 hours is still described as appropriate in hospitalized and severely ill
patients, where observational evidence supports benefit beyond the outpatient window.

Antibiotics have no activity against influenza and are reserved for bacterial complications.

## Where guidance differs

Bodies differ on antiviral use in low-risk outpatients. Some guidance describes it as a reasonable
option for symptom reduction; other guidance and independent evidence reviews are more restrictive,
holding that the effect on symptom duration is modest, the effect on complications and
hospitalization in this group is not well established, and routine use is not justified. Answers
should distinguish the high-risk recommendation, which is consistent, from the low-risk one, which
is not.
