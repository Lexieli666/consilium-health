> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## Why this has its own note

Diabetes is largely self-managed. Most decisions affecting the outcome are made by the person
between appointments, and adherence to glucose-lowering medication is measurably imperfect in
practice. Treating non-adherence as a character issue misses what the evidence shows: cost,
complexity, side effects, low health literacy, depression, and simply forgetting account for most of
it, and each has a different remedy.

## Diabetes self-management education

Structured education and support is described as a core part of care rather than an optional extra,
with specific points at which it is offered: at diagnosis, annually where treatment targets are not
met, when complicating factors develop, and when transitions in care occur. It improves glucose
control and quality of life, and it is consistently underused.

## Making the regimen easier to follow

- **Simplify where possible.** Fewer daily doses and fixed-dose combinations improve adherence.
  Where a regimen has become more complex than the individualized target requires,
  de-intensification is an appropriate response rather than a concession.
- **Name the cost problem out loud.** Cost-related underuse — skipping doses, splitting tablets, not
  filling prescriptions — is common and is rarely volunteered unless asked about directly.
- **Tie doses to existing routines** rather than to clock times, and use pill organizers, blister
  packs or reminders.
- **Anticipate side effects.** Gastrointestinal effects with metformin and genital mycotic infections
  with SGLT-2 inhibitors are common early reasons for silent discontinuation, and both are usually
  manageable if raised.
- **Screen for depression.** It is more common in diabetes and materially reduces self-care; treating
  it improves both.

## Monitoring that is actually used

Glucose monitoring only helps if the readings change something. Guidance describes agreeing in
advance what the person will do in response to particular readings, rather than recording numbers
that are reviewed only at the next appointment. Where continuous monitoring is available, the same
principle applies to time-in-range.

## The review schedule

Long-term glucose control is measured at least twice yearly when stable and at goal, and quarterly
otherwise. Alongside it, ongoing care includes annual eye screening, kidney function and urine
albumin testing, foot examination, blood pressure and lipid review, dental care, and vaccination.
These are separate from glucose control and are frequently the parts that lapse.

## Sick days and travel

Illness raises glucose and changes requirements, and glucose-lowering medication is not simply
stopped because someone is not eating — that combination is a recognized route into a
hyperglycemic emergency. A sick-day plan agreed in advance with the person's own clinician is the
described approach. This system does not provide one, and does not give dosing instructions.
