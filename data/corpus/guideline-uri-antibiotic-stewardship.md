## The default position

Acute upper respiratory infections — the common cold, most sore throats, acute bronchitis, and
uncomplicated acute sinusitis in its first days — are viral in the large majority of cases, and
antibiotics do not shorten them, do not prevent complications at a rate that justifies routine use,
and do cause harm: adverse effects, allergic reactions, disruption of normal flora, *Clostridioides
difficile* infection, and selection for resistant organisms in the individual as well as the
population.

Guidance describes not prescribing antibiotics for these presentations as the active recommendation,
not merely the absence of one. Communication is treated as part of the clinical intervention:
explaining the expected course, naming the duration, describing what would change the plan, and
offering symptomatic measures reduces both prescribing and dissatisfaction, whereas a bare refusal
does neither.

## What does not indicate a bacterial infection

- **Discolored nasal discharge.** Yellow or green discharge is part of the normal viral course.
- **Duration alone within the expected window.** A cold lasting seven to ten days is running its
  course; cough alone persisting two to three weeks after a cold is expected.
- **Low-grade fever** in the first days.
- **Productive cough** in acute bronchitis, which is viral in the overwhelming majority of
  otherwise healthy adults.

## When antibiotics are described as indicated

- **Acute bacterial sinusitis**, identified by one of three patterns rather than by symptoms alone:
  symptoms persisting ten days or more without improvement; severe symptoms with high fever and
  purulent discharge for three to four consecutive days at onset; or the "double worsening" pattern
  in which symptoms improve and then deteriorate.
- **Group A streptococcal pharyngitis**, confirmed by rapid antigen testing or culture rather than
  by appearance. Clinical scoring systems select who to test; they do not replace the test, since
  the features overlap heavily with viral pharyngitis.
- **Pneumonia**, suspected on focal findings, abnormal vital signs, or a compatible chest image —
  a different diagnosis rather than an upper respiratory infection that has gone on too long.
- **Complications** such as peritonsillar abscess, or infection in someone significantly
  immunocompromised.

## Delayed prescribing

A prescription given with instructions to fill it only if symptoms fail to improve or worsen by a
stated point is described as a middle option, reducing antibiotic use substantially compared with
immediate prescribing while maintaining satisfaction. It is described as most useful where the
clinician judges bacterial infection unlikely but the person's expectation or access to follow-up
argues against a plain refusal.

## Where guidance differs

Bodies differ on how far the delayed-prescription strategy should be encouraged. Some describe it as
a recommended tool for reducing immediate prescribing; others are wary, on the grounds that it still
results in a prescription in a substantial fraction of cases and may reinforce the expectation that
these illnesses warrant antibiotics at all. Guidance also differs on sore-throat management, with
some national guidance placing greater weight on clinical scoring and less on universal testing
before treatment.
