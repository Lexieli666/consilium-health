## What it is

Gastroesophageal reflux disease — gastro-oesophageal in British spelling, and abbreviated GERD or
GORD accordingly — is the condition in which stomach contents move back into the esophagus often
enough, or with enough consequence, to cause troublesome symptoms or damage to the esophageal
lining. Some reflux is normal and happens in everyone; what makes it a disease is the symptom burden
or the injury, not the presence of reflux itself.

The usual mechanism is transient relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter, the ring of muscle at
the junction between esophagus (oesophagus) and stomach, sometimes with a hiatus hernia that
displaces the junction and weakens the barrier further.

## How it is usually recognized

The typical symptoms are heartburn — a burning discomfort rising behind the breastbone — and
regurgitation of acid or food into the throat, often worse after meals, on bending forward, and when
lying down. The pattern, particularly its relation to meals and position, is more informative than
the severity.

Less typical presentations include chronic cough, hoarseness, throat clearing, a sensation of a lump
in the throat, dental erosion, and non-cardiac chest pain. These are considerably less specific:
many people with them turn out to have another explanation, which is why attributing them to reflux
without considering alternatives is unreliable and why they respond less predictably to acid
suppression.

## What contributes to it

Excess body weight, particularly abdominal; hiatus hernia; pregnancy; smoking; large or late meals;
and alcohol. Several medications can worsen it, including calcium-channel blockers, nitrates and
others that relax the lower esophageal sphincter. Individual food triggers vary and are far less
consistent between people than is usually assumed, which is the argument for identifying a person's
own triggers rather than applying a standard list.

## Why it is treated

Beyond symptom burden, which can be substantial and affects sleep and quality of life, persistent
acid exposure can cause esophagitis, stricture with narrowing and difficulty swallowing, and
Barrett's esophagus — a change in the lining that carries a small increased risk of esophageal
cancer. That risk is the reason long-standing or alarm-feature-associated reflux is investigated
rather than treated indefinitely on assumption.

## How it is generally managed

Weight management where relevant, meal timing and portion changes, elevating the head of the bed for
night-time symptoms, smoking cessation, and acid-suppressing treatment with a defined trial period
and reassessment rather than open-ended use. Alarm features, endoscopy indications and the step-down
approach are covered by the GERD guideline note in this corpus.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Chest discomfort must not be assumed to be reflux. Discomfort brought on by exertion, or coming with
breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or pain spreading to the arm, neck or jaw, or occurring in someone
with cardiovascular risk factors, requires emergency assessment for a cardiac cause first —
heartburn and cardiac pain are not reliably distinguishable by how they feel. Difficulty swallowing,
painful swallowing, vomiting blood, black tarry stools, or unintentional weight loss also require
prompt medical assessment. The emergency-symptom notes in this corpus govern these situations.
