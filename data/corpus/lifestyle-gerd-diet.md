## The two dietary changes with the best support

Meal size and meal timing carry more weight than food choice, and they are the two most people have
not tried systematically.

A large meal distends the stomach and raises the pressure working against the valve at the top of
it, so the same food divided into smaller portions produces fewer symptoms than it does in one
sitting. Timing matters because lying down removes gravity from the equation: a stomach that is
still full when someone becomes horizontal has nothing holding its contents down. Leaving a gap of
several hours between the last meal and lying down is the change most often described, and for
people whose symptoms are mainly nocturnal it tends to do more than any food substitution.

Weight reduction where body weight is above the healthy range has the strongest supporting evidence
of any lifestyle measure here; the reflux guideline note in this corpus places it among the
recommendations.

## The trigger-food list, and why blanket avoidance is not advised

Coffee, chocolate, alcohol, citrus, tomato, mint, fatty and fried food, spicy food and carbonated
drinks appear on almost every list handed to someone with reflux. The evidence that removing all of
them helps is weak, and the cost is real: a diet stripped of most of what a person enjoys is hard to
sustain and tends to be abandoned wholesale, including the parts that were working.

The approach usually described instead is individual identification. Symptoms are recorded alongside
what was eaten for a couple of weeks, the foods that repeatedly precede symptoms are removed, and
the rest stay. Most people find they have two or three genuine triggers rather than twenty, and the
list is not the same from person to person.

Two items have a mechanism behind them that generalizes a little better than the rest. Alcohol
relaxes the valve and increases acid exposure, and carbonated drinks add gas that distends the
stomach directly.

## Fat, and what it actually does

Fatty meals slow stomach emptying, which extends the window during which reflux can occur. This is
part of why a heavy late dinner is a reliable provoker where the same food at midday is not — the
effect is as much about how long the meal stays in the stomach as about the fat itself.

## What is not established

Alkaline water, apple cider vinegar, aloe preparations and similar remedies do not have evidence
comparable to the measures above. Milk gives brief relief and is followed by a rebound in many
people. Chewing gum after a meal increases saliva and has some supporting evidence, which puts it
among the more plausible of the folk measures rather than among the established ones.

Symptoms that persist despite these changes, or that come with difficulty swallowing, unintended
weight loss, vomiting or bleeding, are a reason for assessment rather than further dietary
adjustment.
