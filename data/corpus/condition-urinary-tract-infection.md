## What it is

A urinary tract infection is bacterial infection of the urinary system. Most involve the bladder and
urethra — a lower tract infection, or cystitis. Infection reaching the kidney is pyelonephritis, an
upper tract infection, and it is a materially different and more serious illness rather than a worse
version of the same one. *Escherichia coli* causes the large majority.

The distinction that drives management is between **uncomplicated** infection — in a non-pregnant
adult with a normal urinary tract and normal immune function — and **complicated** infection, which
covers infection in pregnancy, in men, in people with structural or functional urinary abnormality,
catheters, kidney stones, diabetes with poor control, or immunosuppression. The same organism in the
same place is treated differently depending on which category applies.

## How it is usually recognized

Lower tract infection: burning or pain on urination, urinary frequency and urgency, passing small
volumes, lower abdominal discomfort, and cloudy or strong-smelling urine. Blood in the urine occurs
and is not by itself a sign of something worse in this context, though it warrants follow-up to
confirm resolution.

Upper tract infection adds fever, chills, flank or back pain, nausea and vomiting, and a general
sense of being unwell. Fever and flank pain are the features that move the presentation out of the
routine category.

Presentation is frequently atypical in older adults, where confusion, falls or functional decline
may dominate. This is genuinely difficult territory: asymptomatic bacteria in the urine are common
in older adults and generally not treated, so a positive urine test in a confused older person is
not on its own a diagnosis, and treating it as one both misses the real cause and contributes to
resistance.

## What contributes to it

Female anatomy, with a shorter urethra; sexual activity; certain contraceptives including
spermicides and diaphragms; post-menopausal changes in vaginal flora; incomplete bladder emptying;
urinary catheters; kidney stones; diabetes; and previous infections. In men, infections are less
common and more often associated with prostatic enlargement or another structural factor, which is
why they are treated as complicated by default.

## How it is generally managed

Short-course antibiotic treatment for uncomplicated lower tract infection, guided by local
resistance patterns, with urine culture reserved for cases where the diagnosis is uncertain, the
infection is complicated or recurrent, or the first treatment has failed. Fluids and symptomatic
relief support the treatment without replacing it. Antibiotic choice, culture indications and
recurrence prevention are covered by the urinary tract infection guideline note in this corpus.
Doses are outside the scope of this system.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Fever with flank or back pain, shaking chills, vomiting preventing fluid intake, confusion,
symptoms in pregnancy, symptoms in someone with a single kidney or a transplant, or symptoms that
worsen despite 48 hours of treatment — these need prompt assessment. Fever, rapid heart rate,
low blood pressure and confusion together suggest sepsis and are an emergency; the emergency-symptom
notes in this corpus govern that situation.
