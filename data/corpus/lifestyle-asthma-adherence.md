## Technique is the adherence problem people do not know they have

An inhaler taken faithfully every day with poor technique delivers a fraction of what it should, and
the record shows perfect adherence. Incorrect technique is common, it persists for years unless
someone checks it, and it is the first thing usually reviewed when treatment appears to be failing —
ahead of changing the drug.

The errors depend on the device. Pressurized inhalers are most often let down by poor coordination
between actuation and breath, and by breathing in too fast; dry powder inhalers need a fast, deep
breath in, which is the opposite instruction, and they fail when someone breathes out into the
device and dampens the powder. Someone using both device types is being asked to do two
contradictory things, which is worth naming rather than assuming.

A **spacer** used with a pressurized inhaler removes the coordination problem almost entirely and
improves delivery to the lungs. Technique is worth having checked at review appointments rather than
assumed, and a demonstration is more useful than a leaflet.

## Rinsing, and the side effect that drives people to stop

Rinsing the mouth and spitting after an inhaled corticosteroid reduces hoarseness and oral thrush.
These are minor problems clinically and major ones practically, because they are among the reasons
people quietly stop taking a controller inhaler.

## The controller and reliever confusion

The most consequential misunderstanding in asthma is between an inhaler taken every day whether or
not there are symptoms and one taken for immediate relief. Because the daily inhaler produces no
sensation and the relief inhaler produces an immediate one, the daily inhaler is the one dropped
first when someone feels well — which is precisely when it is working.

Knowing which device is which, by name and by appearance, is worth confirming explicitly. Rising use
of a relief inhaler is a signal that the underlying condition is not controlled rather than a sign
that the relief inhaler is doing its job; the asthma guideline note in this corpus covers what
modern guidance says about reliever-only treatment.

## The written action plan

A personalized written plan states what to take daily, what to do when symptoms worsen, and at what
point to seek help. It converts a decision made while breathless into one made in advance, and
having a copy where it can be found — on a phone, and with family — is part of the point.

## Refills, routines and the practical rest

A prescription collected less often than the dose requires is a straightforward indicator of missed
doses, and is worth tracking as information rather than treated as an accusation. Anchoring the
daily inhaler to an existing habit such as brushing teeth is the most durable reminder most people
find. Checking dose counters before a trip, keeping a relief inhaler where it will actually be, and
reducing exposure to identified triggers at home all belong to the same practical category.
