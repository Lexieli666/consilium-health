## What it is

Type 2 diabetes is a disorder of glucose regulation in which the body's tissues respond poorly to
insulin — insulin resistance — and the pancreas cannot sustain the increased output that resistance
demands. The result is a blood glucose level that stays above the normal range. It differs from type
1 diabetes, in which insulin-producing cells are destroyed by an autoimmune process and insulin is
absent rather than ineffective. The distinction matters because type 1 can present in adults, where
it is sometimes mislabeled.

The condition develops gradually, over years. The period of raised-but-not-diabetic glucose that
precedes it — prediabetes — is a recognized state with its own management, and it is the point at
which intensive lifestyle change has the largest effect.

## How it is usually recognized

Many people have no symptoms at diagnosis and are identified by screening. Where symptoms are
present, the classic ones are increased thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss,
fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds and recurrent infections. Because onset is gradual,
complications are sometimes already present at the time of diagnosis — which is the argument for
screening rather than waiting for symptoms.

Diagnostic thresholds and screening criteria are covered by the diabetes screening guideline note in
this corpus.

## What contributes to it

Excess body weight, particularly around the abdomen; physical inactivity; a family history of the
condition; increasing age; a history of gestational diabetes or of polycystic ovary syndrome; and
higher background risk in several racial and ethnic groups. Certain medications raise risk,
including long-term corticosteroids and some antipsychotics. Obstructive sleep apnea is commonly
present alongside it and worsens glucose control independently.

## Why it is treated

Sustained high glucose damages small and large blood vessels. The small-vessel complications are
retinal disease, kidney disease and nerve damage, including the foot problems that follow loss of
protective sensation. The large-vessel complications are heart attack, stroke and peripheral
arterial disease, which account for most of the excess mortality associated with the condition. Management therefore addresses blood pressure,
lipids and smoking alongside glucose, and modern treatment choices are made partly on cardiovascular
and kidney grounds rather than on glucose alone.

## How it is generally managed

Structured lifestyle intervention — dietary change, physical activity, weight management — together
with medication chosen according to which other conditions are present. Ongoing care includes
periodic measurement of long-term glucose control, annual eye screening, kidney function and urine
albumin testing, foot examination, and management of cardiovascular risk factors. Targets, testing
intervals and drug classes are covered by the diabetes guideline notes in this corpus.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Very high glucose with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep or rapid breathing, drowsiness or
confusion may indicate a hyperglycemic emergency and requires immediate medical attention. Symptoms
of low blood sugar — shakiness, sweating, confusion, difficulty concentrating — in someone taking
insulin or an agent that stimulates insulin release require prompt treatment, and loss of
consciousness or seizure is an emergency. The emergency-symptom notes in this corpus govern these
situations.
