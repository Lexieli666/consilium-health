## Why sleep gets its own note here

Sleep and migraine influence each other in both directions, which is unusual among the commonly
cited triggers. Disturbed sleep makes attacks more likely; attacks disturb sleep; and sleep is also
one of the more reliable ways an attack ends. A change in sleep pattern is among the most frequently
identified triggers people report, and it is one of the few that is straightforwardly modifiable.

## Regularity matters more than duration

The pattern most often described is that change, rather than any particular amount, is what provokes
attacks. Too little sleep triggers them; so does sleeping in. This is why weekend headaches are such
a recognizable pattern — a late Friday night followed by a long Saturday lie-in shifts sleep timing
in both directions across two days, and often takes a relaxation in caffeine intake with it.

The practical implication runs against instinct. Recovering lost sleep by extending the weekend
morning is the move most likely to produce an attack, and holding the wake time steady, then
catching up with a short early-afternoon nap if needed, is the alternative usually suggested.

## Sleep as an abortive

Many people find that sleep ends an attack, and that the attack lifts on waking. This is worth
knowing because it conflicts with the general advice about protecting night-time sleep pressure:
during an attack, lying down in a dark quiet room and sleeping is a reasonable response rather than
a habit to be resisted. The regularity advice applies to ordinary days, not to the day of an attack.

## Where the two conditions overlap

Insomnia and migraine occur together more often than either alone would predict, and so does
obstructive sleep apnea (apnoea). Two patterns are worth recognizing because they change what should
be looked at rather than what should be done at home: headache present on waking most mornings, and
loud snoring with witnessed pauses in breathing or heavy daytime sleepiness. Both are reasons for
assessment rather than for adjusting habits.

Frequent night waking with headache, or headache that repeatedly wakes someone from sleep, is
likewise a pattern to raise with a clinician rather than to manage with sleep habits.

## Caffeine sits inside the sleep question

Caffeine is a common component of acute headache treatment and also a determinant of sleep quality,
so the two cannot be adjusted independently. A steady daily intake, taken early in the day, avoids
both the withdrawal headache that follows an abrupt drop and the sleep fragmentation that follows a
late dose. Sudden changes in either direction are the part most often associated with attacks. The
migraine guideline note in this corpus covers acute and preventive treatment, including what happens
when acute treatment is used too often.
