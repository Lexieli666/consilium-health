## The empiric approach

For an adult with typical symptoms — heartburn and regurgitation — and no alarm features, the
initial approach to gastroesophageal reflux disease (gastro-oesophageal in British spelling) is an
one guidance describes: an empiric eight-week trial of acid suppression with a proton pump inhibitor
taken before a meal, rather than investigation first. Typical symptoms plus response to acid suppression is informative
enough in this group that endoscopy first would change management in few people. Timing matters as
much as the agent: these drugs act on activated pumps, so a dose taken with or after food is a
common reason for apparent non-response. Doses are outside the scope of this note.

## Lifestyle measures

Weight reduction where body weight is above the healthy range has the strongest supporting evidence.
Others are elevating the head of the bed for people with night-time symptoms, avoiding meals within
two to three hours of lying down, stopping smoking, and avoiding individually identified trigger
foods rather than applying a blanket elimination diet, which is poorly supported.

## Alarm features that change the approach

Prompt endoscopy rather than an empiric trial where any of the following are present:

- Difficulty swallowing, or pain on swallowing.
- Unintentional weight loss.
- Gastrointestinal bleeding — vomiting blood, or black tarry stools.
- Iron-deficiency anemia (anaemia).
- Persistent vomiting.
- A palpable mass, or a family history of upper gastrointestinal cancer.
- New onset of symptoms at an older age.

## After the trial

Where symptoms respond, the next step is stepping down to the lowest effective therapy, or to
on-demand use, rather than continuing indefinitely at the initial intensity. Where symptoms do not
respond, adherence and timing relative to meals are confirmed first, then referral for endoscopy
and, in selected people, esophageal pH or pH-impedance testing performed off therapy to establish
whether acid reflux is in fact the cause. A substantial share of non-responders turn out to have
functional heartburn, reflux hypersensitivity, or a motility disorder — conditions that more acid
suppression will not improve, which is why escalating the dose indefinitely is not the answer to
non-response.

## Long-term therapy

Where long-term acid suppression is genuinely needed — erosive esophagitis (oesophagitis), Barrett's
esophagus (oesophagus), or symptom recurrence on withdrawal — it is continued at the lowest
effective intensity with periodic review, rather than renewed indefinitely without reassessment.

## An important overlap

Chest discomfort cannot be attributed to reflux without considering a cardiac cause, particularly
where it is exertional, associated with breathlessness or sweating, or occurring in someone with
cardiovascular risk factors. Cardiac assessment takes precedence, and the emergency-symptom notes
elsewhere in this corpus govern that case.
