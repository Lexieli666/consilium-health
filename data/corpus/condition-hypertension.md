## What it is

Hypertension is persistently raised pressure in the arteries, sustained over time rather than on a
single occasion. Blood pressure is written as two numbers: the systolic pressure, generated when the
heart contracts, over the diastolic pressure, present between beats. Both are measured in
millimeters of mercury.

Pressure varies continuously through the day — with activity, stress, pain, caffeine, sleep and time
of day, typically lowest during sleep and rising before waking. That variation is why a diagnosis
rests on repeated measurements rather than one reading, and why a single high reading in a clinic is
not itself a diagnosis.

## How it is usually recognized

Hypertension is typically without symptoms and is most often found on routine measurement rather
than because someone felt unwell. That is the main reason screening for it exists at all. Where
symptoms such as headache, visual disturbance or nosebleed are attributed to raised blood pressure,
they usually reflect either a very high pressure or an unrelated cause — the belief that ordinary
hypertension can be felt leads people to treat by symptom rather than by measurement, which does not
work.

## What contributes to it

Most cases have no single identifiable cause and reflect a combination of age, genetic
predisposition, excess body weight, high dietary sodium, low potassium intake, physical inactivity,
alcohol intake, poor sleep including untreated sleep apnea (apnoea), and chronic stress. Arterial
stiffening with age is why systolic pressure tends to rise across the lifespan while diastolic
pressure often falls after middle age.

A minority of cases have an identifiable secondary cause — kidney disease, renal artery narrowing,
primary aldosteronism, thyroid or adrenal disorders, obstructive sleep apnea, and certain medications
including combined oral contraceptives, decongestants, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and
some stimulants. Investigation for a secondary cause is usually considered when hypertension appears
at a young age, is unusually severe, responds poorly to treatment, or worsens abruptly after a period
of stability.

## Why it is treated

Sustained high pressure damages arteries over years, and the conditions that follow are the reason
for treating a symptomless number: stroke, heart attack, heart failure, chronic kidney disease,
retinal damage, and cognitive decline. The benefit of treatment is measured in reduced rates of those
events, not in how the person feels — which is also why adherence is difficult, since a treatment
that produces no felt improvement and occasional side effects is easy to stop.

## How it is generally managed

Lifestyle measures — dietary sodium reduction, a diet pattern high in vegetables, fruit and whole
grains, regular activity, weight management, limiting alcohol, and stopping smoking — apply to
everyone. Drug treatment is added according to the level of risk. Diagnostic thresholds, treatment
targets, drug classes and follow-up intervals are covered by the hypertension guideline notes in
this corpus.

## When urgent assessment is advised

A very high reading accompanied by chest pain, breathlessness, severe headache, visual loss,
confusion, weakness or numbness on one side, or difficulty speaking is an emergency and requires
immediate medical attention rather than a routine appointment. The emergency-symptom notes in this
corpus govern those situations.
