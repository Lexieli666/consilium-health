## Call emergency services immediately

Sepsis is the body's dysregulated response to infection causing organ dysfunction. In someone with a
known or suspected infection, any of the following warrants emergency assessment:

- Confusion, disorientation, slurred speech, or unusual drowsiness.
- Very fast breathing, or breathlessness.
- A fast heart rate, or dizziness on standing.
- Not passing urine for many hours.
- Skin that is mottled, ashen, very pale or blue; or a rash that does not fade when pressed.
- Shivering, or feeling very cold, or a very high or very low temperature.
- Severe muscle pain, or a feeling of being more unwell than ever before.

In a child, additional signs include fast breathing, a fit or convulsion, mottled or ashen skin, a
rash that does not fade, lethargy or difficulty waking, feeling abnormally cold, not feeding, no
urine for many hours, and repeated vomiting.

## Why the phrase "just say sepsis" exists

Sepsis progresses over hours, and survival falls with every hour of delay in recognition and
treatment. Its early presentation is nonspecific and resembles a bad case of an ordinary infection,
so it is regularly missed at first contact. Guidance encourages the person or family to raise the
possibility explicitly — asking "could this be sepsis?" — because that question reliably prompts a
structured assessment, and because the family's sense that the person is unusually unwell is
diagnostically valuable and often the earliest signal available.

## Where it comes from

Any infection can cause it. Common sources are the lungs, the urinary tract, the abdomen, and skin
or wounds, including surgical wounds and intravenous lines. The original infection may be minor and
may already be treated.

## Who is at higher risk

Infants and adults over 65; pregnancy and the six weeks after; people with diabetes, chronic liver
or kidney disease, or cancer; people on chemotherapy, steroids or other immunosuppressants; people
without a functioning spleen; people with indwelling lines or catheters; and anyone with a recent
operation or invasive procedure. In these groups the threshold for escalation is lower still, and
fever may be absent.

## What not to do

Do not wait to see whether a fever settles overnight in someone with these features. Do not be
reassured by a normal temperature: a low temperature is as concerning as a high one, and older
adults may have neither. Do not delay because antibiotics have already been started — sepsis can
develop despite them, and an infection that was responding can still progress if the source has not
been controlled.
