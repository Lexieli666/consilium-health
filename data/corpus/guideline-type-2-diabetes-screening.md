## Who is screened

Diabetes association guidance screens all adults from age 35, and screens younger adults who are
overweight or obese and have at least one additional risk factor — a first-degree relative with
diabetes, a high-risk race or ethnicity, established cardiovascular disease, high blood pressure,
low HDL cholesterol or raised triglycerides, polycystic ovary syndrome, physical inactivity, or a
history of gestational diabetes. A lower body-mass-index threshold applies to people of Asian
ancestry, in whom risk rises at a lower body weight.

Where screening is normal, the usual repeat interval is three years, and shorter where risk factors
accumulate or weight increases.

## Diagnostic thresholds

Any one of the following meets the diagnostic threshold:

- **HbA1c** (glycated hemoglobin, also written haemoglobin A1c) at or above 6.5 percent.
- **Fasting plasma glucose** at or above 126 mg/dL, after at least eight hours without caloric
  intake.
- **Two-hour plasma glucose** at or above 200 mg/dL during a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test.
- **Random plasma glucose** at or above 200 mg/dL in a person with classic symptoms of
  hyperglycemia or a hyperglycemic crisis.

A second test — either a repeat of the same test or a different one — confirms the result, unless
the person has unequivocal hyperglycemia with classic symptoms, in which case treatment is not
delayed for confirmation. Where two different tests are done and only one is above threshold, the
usual approach is to repeat the one that was abnormal.

## The intermediate category

Values above normal but below the diagnostic thresholds are classified as prediabetes: HbA1c 5.7 to
6.4 percent, fasting plasma glucose 100 to 125 mg/dL, or two-hour glucose 140 to 199 mg/dL. This
group is offered structured lifestyle intervention and monitored annually, on the basis that
progression to diabetes is common but not inevitable and that intensive lifestyle change reduces it.

## Where guidance differs

The screening population is a genuine point of disagreement. Diabetes association guidance describes
screening all adults from age 35 regardless of weight; preventive-services guidance restricts
routine screening to adults aged 35 to 70 who are overweight or obese, on the grounds that this is
the group in whom screening trials show benefit. The two positions imply substantially different
numbers of people tested, and an answer about "who should be screened" should say which it is
describing.

## A caveat on HbA1c

HbA1c is unreliable wherever red-cell turnover is altered — hemoglobin variants, recent transfusion,
iron-deficiency anemia (anaemia), pregnancy, hemolysis, and advanced kidney disease — because it
measures the fraction of hemoglobin that has been glycated over the red cell's lifespan. Where those
conditions are present, glucose-based criteria are used instead. Assay standardization also matters:
point-of-care devices are not universally accepted for diagnosis.
