> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## Call emergency services immediately

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing, potentially fatal allergic reaction involving more than one
body system. Suspect it when symptoms begin within minutes to a couple of hours of an exposure and
involve any of:

- **Airway** — swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, a tight throat, difficulty swallowing,
  hoarse voice, or stridor.
- **Breathing** — breathlessness, wheeze, persistent cough, or chest tightness.
- **Circulation** — dizziness, faintness, collapse, pallor, or a weak rapid pulse.
- **Skin** — widespread hives, flushing, or swelling, usually but not always present.
- **Gut** — severe abdominal pain, vomiting, or diarrhea alongside any of the above.

Skin involvement is absent in a meaningful minority of cases. Its absence does not exclude
anaphylaxis, and waiting for a rash before acting is a recognized cause of delay.

## Adrenaline first, and without delay

Where an adrenaline auto-injector is available and anaphylaxis is suspected, guidance across all
bodies is that it is used immediately, into the outer thigh, before or while emergency services are
called. Delay in giving adrenaline is the factor most consistently associated with fatal outcomes.
Antihistamines and inhalers do not treat anaphylaxis — they act on some symptoms and not on the
airway swelling or circulatory collapse that cause death — and using them instead of adrenaline
loses the time that matters.

A second dose may be needed if there is no improvement after about five minutes. This system does
not give dosing instructions; the device's own labeled instructions and emergency dispatchers do.

## Positioning matters

Someone with anaphylaxis is kept lying flat with legs raised if they feel faint, or sitting up if
breathing is difficult. Standing up or being walked around can cause sudden collapse, and doing so
has been associated with deaths. If unresponsive and breathing, the recovery position is used.

## Everyone goes to hospital, even after improvement

Symptoms can return hours after an apparently complete recovery — a biphasic reaction — so
assessment and a period of observation follow every episode, including those that responded fully to
adrenaline.

## Common triggers

Foods, most often peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, egg, sesame and wheat; insect stings;
medications, particularly antibiotics and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; latex; and, less
commonly, exercise in combination with a food. A previous mild reaction does not predict a mild
next one, and the severity of an episode depends on the dose, the route and the person's state at
the time as much as on the trigger itself.

This system does not diagnose. Any presentation matching this note is escalated, and no answer it
produces is a substitute for emergency assessment.
