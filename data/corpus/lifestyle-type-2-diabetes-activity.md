> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## What is generally recommended

At least 150 minutes per week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity spread over at least three
days, with no more than two consecutive days without activity, plus resistance training on two to
three non-consecutive days. The spacing instruction is specific to diabetes rather than generic:
the improvement in insulin sensitivity after a session lasts roughly 24 to 72 hours, so activity
clustered into a single weekend leaves most of the week without it.

## Why activity works here beyond weight

Muscle contraction moves glucose into cells by a route that does not require insulin, which is why
activity lowers glucose in people whose tissues respond poorly to insulin. That effect is
independent of whether weight changes, and it is the reason activity is described as valuable even
when the scale does not move — a point worth making, since the scale is what most people use to
judge whether their effort worked.

## Resistance training

Resistance training improves glucose control, preserves muscle mass, and matters more with age and
during weight reduction, when muscle is otherwise lost alongside fat. It is described alongside
aerobic work rather than as an alternative, and the combination outperforms either alone.

## Breaking up sitting

Interrupting prolonged sitting every 30 minutes with a few minutes of light activity is described
as improving glucose control, and it is a separate recommendation from the weekly total. For someone
with a desk-bound job this is often the more achievable of the two.

## Low blood sugar and exercise

For people using insulin or an agent that stimulates insulin release, activity can cause low blood
sugar during or for hours afterward, including overnight. Checking glucose before and after activity,
carrying fast-acting carbohydrate, and agreeing an adjustment plan in advance with their own
clinician are the described precautions. This system does not give medication or dose adjustments.

For people not on those agents, exercise-induced low blood sugar is uncommon.

## Where caution or assessment comes first

- **Established cardiovascular disease, or symptoms suggesting it** — assessment before vigorous
  activity.
- **Proliferative retinopathy** — vigorous, high-impact or straining activity may be restricted
  because of bleeding risk.
- **Peripheral neuropathy** — foot care, appropriate footwear and daily foot inspection matter, and
  non-weight-bearing options such as cycling or swimming may be preferred where there is ulceration
  or loss of protective sensation.
- **Autonomic neuropathy** — heart-rate response to exercise is unreliable as an intensity guide,
  and perceived exertion is used instead.
- **Very high glucose with ketones** — activity is deferred until that is corrected.
