> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## What is generally recommended

The commonly stated target for adults is 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic
activity, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination, plus muscle-strengthening
activity on two or more days. For blood pressure specifically, the aerobic component carries most of
the evidence, and the reduction it produces is comparable in size to what a single
antihypertensive agent achieves in some people.

Moderate intensity means breathing harder and able to talk but not sing — brisk walking, cycling on
the flat, swimming, dancing, gardening that raises the heart rate. Vigorous means being unable to
say more than a few words without pausing.

## The part that matters most

Sessions can be accumulated across the week in whatever blocks fit, and short bouts count. The
older advice that only sustained ten-minute blocks counted has been dropped, and the practical
consequence is that stairs, brisk walks between tasks and short bursts of activity all contribute.

The largest single gain is from moving from doing nothing to doing something. The dose-response
curve is steepest at the low end, so someone currently inactive gains more from the first 60 minutes
a week than an already-active person gains from the last 60.

## Strength training

Resistance training two or more days per week is described as lowering blood pressure independently
of aerobic activity, and the two together are better than either alone. Isometric exercise — such as
sustained handgrip or wall-sit protocols — has attracted attention for a comparatively large effect
on blood pressure in trials, though the evidence base is smaller than for aerobic work and it is
usually described as an adjunct rather than a replacement.

## Reducing sedentary time

Prolonged uninterrupted sitting is associated with worse cardiovascular outcomes partly
independently of how much a person exercises. Breaking up long periods of sitting is described as a
separate target from the weekly activity total, not a substitute for it.

## Building it sustainably

Starting below the target and increasing gradually is more effective than beginning at the target,
because the failure mode is not insufficient ambition but abandonment. Activity that fits an
existing routine, and that the person is willing to keep doing, outperforms a theoretically superior
program that stops after three weeks.

## When medical review comes first

Anyone with chest pain on exertion, breathlessness disproportionate to effort, fainting, palpitations
with exercise, known heart disease, or very high uncontrolled blood pressure should be assessed
before starting vigorous activity. Anyone who develops chest discomfort, severe breathlessness or
faintness during exercise should stop and seek assessment. The emergency-symptom notes in this
corpus govern those situations.
