## What counts as chronic insomnia

Chronic insomnia disorder is difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early,
occurring at least three nights per week for at least three months, despite adequate opportunity to
sleep, and accompanied by daytime consequences — fatigue, impaired attention, mood disturbance, or
impaired performance. The daytime component is part of the definition: short sleep without daytime
consequences is not a disorder, and treating it as one medicalizes normal variation in sleep need.

Assessment includes a sleep diary over one to two weeks, screening for the conditions that commonly
present as insomnia — obstructive sleep apnea (apnoea), restless legs syndrome, circadian rhythm
disorders, depression, anxiety, pain, and substance or medication effects — and review of caffeine,
alcohol, nicotine and shift patterns. Alcohol is worth singling out: it shortens sleep latency and
then fragments the second half of the night, so it is often reported as a remedy by someone whose
sleep it is degrading.

## The first-line recommendation

Both sleep medicine and internal medicine guidance describes cognitive behavioral therapy for
insomnia as first-line for chronic insomnia in adults, ahead of medication. Its effects persist after treatment
ends, whereas pharmacological effects generally do not, and it avoids the harms associated with
long-term hypnotic use.

The components are delivered together as a package over roughly four to eight sessions:

- **Stimulus control** — using the bed only for sleep, leaving the bed when unable to sleep, so the
  bed stops being a cue for wakefulness.
- **Sleep restriction** — limiting time in bed toward actual sleep time, then extending it as sleep
  consolidates. Counterintuitive, and the component people most often abandon early.
- **Cognitive restructuring** of the beliefs about sleep that maintain arousal, including the
  catastrophizing about tomorrow that keeps someone awake tonight.
- **Sleep hygiene education**, necessary but insufficient on its own. Guidance is explicit that
  sleep hygiene advice alone is not effective treatment for chronic insomnia — a frequent
  misunderstanding, and the reason that "I already tried sleep hygiene" is not evidence that this
  therapy has been tried.
- **Relaxation training.**

Digitally delivered and brief formats are reasonable where in-person therapy is not accessible,
which is the practical constraint most often cited against the recommendation.

## Where medication sits

Pharmacotherapy is second-line: for people in whom cognitive behavioral therapy is unavailable, has
been tried without adequate response, or where short-term treatment is needed alongside it. Shared
decision-making, the shortest appropriate duration, and a plan for reassessment and discontinuation
agreed at the point of starting rather than later. Antihistamine-based over-the-counter sleep aids
have limited evidence and notable anticholinergic side effects, particularly in older adults.
