## What it is

Hypothyroidism is insufficient production of thyroid hormone. The thyroid gland produces mainly
thyroxine, which is converted in the tissues to the more active triiodothyronine; together they set
the metabolic rate of most tissues in the body. The pituitary gland monitors the level and releases
thyroid-stimulating hormone in response, so a failing thyroid produces a rising stimulating hormone
before the thyroid hormone level itself falls out of range. That relationship is why the stimulating
hormone is the first test rather than the hormone it regulates.

Primary hypothyroidism, where the gland itself is at fault, accounts for the great majority. Central
hypothyroidism, where the pituitary or hypothalamus is at fault, is uncommon and inverts the usual
test pattern — which is why a low thyroid hormone with a normal or low stimulating hormone is not
simply a milder version of the same thing.

## How it is usually recognized

Symptoms are nonspecific and develop slowly: fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation,
dry skin, hair thinning, muscle aches, heavy or irregular periods, low mood, and slowed thinking.
Each is common in people without thyroid disease, which cuts both ways — the condition is missed
because the symptoms are unremarkable, and it is also over-diagnosed as the explanation for symptoms
that have another cause. Confirming it on repeated blood testing, rather than treating a single
result or a symptom pattern, is what separates the two.

## What contributes to it

Autoimmune thyroiditis is the most common cause where iodine intake is adequate; worldwide, iodine
deficiency remains a major cause. Also: thyroid surgery, radioactive iodine treatment, radiotherapy
to the neck, several medications including lithium and amiodarone, and the recovery phase after
some forms of thyroiditis. It is more common in women and becomes more common with age, and it
clusters with other autoimmune conditions including type 1 diabetes and celiac disease.

## Why it is treated

Untreated overt hypothyroidism affects lipid levels, cardiac function, cognition, fertility and
pregnancy outcomes. In pregnancy it is treated to a tighter standard, because thyroid hormone is
needed for fetal neurological development and requirements rise early — a reason that anyone on
treatment who becomes pregnant is reviewed promptly rather than at the next routine interval.

## How it is generally managed

Levothyroxine replacement, taken consistently with respect to food and separated from agents that
impair absorption, with the dose set by the stimulating hormone level and rechecked six to eight
weeks after any change. Testing intervals, absorption issues and the unresolved question of treating
subclinical disease are covered by the hypothyroidism guideline note in this corpus.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Severe hypothyroidism with confusion, marked drowsiness, very low body temperature or collapse is a
rare emergency requiring immediate care. More commonly, new chest pain or palpitations after
starting or increasing replacement warrants prompt assessment, since the increase in metabolic
demand can unmask coronary disease.
