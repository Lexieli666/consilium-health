## What secondary prevention means

Secondary prevention is the set of measures used for someone who already has established coronary
artery disease — a previous heart attack, a revascularization procedure, or angiographic disease —
to reduce the chance of a further event. It is a package rather than a single intervention: no one
element carries the effect, and the benefit measured in trials comes from applying them together.
Partial application is the common failure, and it is usually the non-drug elements that are dropped.

## The components

- **Antiplatelet therapy** — long-term single antiplatelet therapy for most people, with a defined
  period of dual antiplatelet therapy after an acute coronary syndrome or stent placement, its
  duration individualized against bleeding risk.
- **High-intensity statin therapy**. United States guidance describes the intensity; European
  guidance describes an LDL goal instead. Where the response is inadequate, additional
  non-statin agents are added rather than the statin abandoned.
- **Blood-pressure control**, with a target below 130/80 mm Hg for most people.
- **Renin-angiotensin blockade** where diabetes, reduced left ventricular function, or chronic
  kidney disease is also present.
- **Beta-blockade** after myocardial infarction, with the strongest and longest-standing evidence
  where left ventricular function is reduced.
- **Cardiac rehabilitation referral** — a structured program of supervised exercise, education and
  risk-factor management, and consistently one of the most underused elements relative to the
  strength of the evidence behind it.
- **Smoking cessation**, the single largest modifiable factor, with combined behavioral and
  pharmacologic support more effective than advice alone.
- **Annual influenza vaccination**, included as a cardiovascular measure rather than only a
  respiratory one.
- **Structured management of diabetes** where present, preferring agents with demonstrated
  cardiovascular benefit.

## Review interval

Review at least annually once the regimen is stable, with earlier review after any change in
symptoms, after a hospital admission, and four to twelve weeks after any change in lipid-lowering
intensity. Each review is an opportunity to check adherence, which falls measurably in the year
after an event as the acute experience recedes.

## What warrants urgent reassessment

New, worsening, or rest-onset chest discomfort in someone with known coronary disease is not a
routine review matter and requires urgent assessment. A pattern of angina that is becoming more
frequent, more easily provoked, or longer-lasting is a change in category rather than a change in
degree. The emergency-symptom notes elsewhere in this corpus govern these situations, and this note
does not substitute for them.
