> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## Who this applies to

Uncomplicated infection means acute cystitis in a non-pregnant adult with a structurally and
functionally normal urinary tract and normal immune function. Everything outside that definition —
pregnancy, men, catheters, structural abnormality, stones, immunosuppression, suspected kidney
involvement — is managed as complicated infection, on a different pathway with different antibiotic
choices and durations.

## Diagnosis without over-testing

In a person with typical symptoms and no vaginal discharge or irritation, the probability of
infection is high enough that empiric treatment is appropriate without culture. Urine dipstick adds
little in that group — a positive result confirms what was already likely, and a negative one does
not reliably exclude it. Dipstick is more useful where symptoms are equivocal.

Urine culture is reserved for cases where it changes management: suspected pyelonephritis, pregnancy,
complicated infection, recurrent infection, symptoms persisting or recurring within a few weeks of
treatment, or recent antibiotic exposure or travel making resistance likely.

## Treatment

Short-course therapy with an agent that concentrates in urine and has minimal effect on gut flora is
preferred over broader agents. Nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole where local resistance
is low, and fosfomycin are the commonly described first-line options; fluoroquinolones are
explicitly reserved because of serious adverse effects and their value in other infections. Local
resistance patterns drive the choice, and a first-line agent chosen without reference to them is a
guess. Beta-lactams are described as alternatives with somewhat lower efficacy. Doses and durations
are outside the scope of this system.

Symptoms typically improve within 48 hours. Failure to improve is a reason to reassess and culture,
not to extend the same course.

## Asymptomatic bacteriuria

Bacteria in the urine without symptoms is common — particularly in older adults, people with
catheters, and people with diabetes — and treating it does not improve outcomes while it does drive
resistance and adverse effects. Screening and treatment are recommended only in pregnancy and before
urologic procedures that breach the mucosa. This is one of the clearest "do not treat" positions in
antimicrobial guidance, and one of the most frequently disregarded.

## Recurrence

Recurrent infection is conventionally two or more in six months or three or more in a year. Measures
described include behavioral modification, vaginal estrogen after menopause, and — where recurrence
is frequent and disabling — antibiotic prophylaxis, either continuous or after intercourse, weighed
against resistance. Cranberry products and increased fluid intake are described as having limited
and mixed evidence.

## Where guidance differs

Bodies differ on the role of non-antibiotic first-line management for mild cystitis. Some European
guidance describes offering symptomatic treatment with delayed or no antibiotics as a shared
decision, on evidence that a proportion of episodes resolve without antibiotics; other guidance
retains immediate antibiotics as the default, citing symptom duration and the risk of progression to
kidney involvement. The disagreement is about how much antibiotic exposure is worth avoiding, not
about the facts.
