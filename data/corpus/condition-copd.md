## What it is

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is persistent airflow limitation caused by long-term damage to
the airways and the lung tissue itself. Two processes usually coexist: chronic inflammation and
narrowing of the small airways, and emphysema, the destruction of the alveolar walls across which
gas is exchanged. The limitation is persistent rather than variable, which is the main contrast with
asthma, although the two overlap in some people and the overlap is treated as its own situation.

The damage is not reversible. Treatment reduces symptoms, improves exercise capacity and reduces
exacerbations; stopping the exposure that caused it is what changes the trajectory.

## How it is usually recognized

Breathlessness on exertion that has worsened gradually over years, chronic cough, and sputum
production, in someone with a relevant exposure history. People commonly attribute the early stage
to age or being out of shape and reduce their activity to match, which conceals the progression —
so the first presentation is often later than the first symptom, sometimes at an exacerbation.

The diagnosis requires spirometry and is not made on symptoms alone; the COPD guideline note in this
corpus covers the threshold and the assessment.

## What contributes to it

Tobacco smoking is the dominant cause in high-income countries. Also relevant: biomass fuel smoke
from indoor cooking and heating, occupational dust, vapors and fumes, air pollution, childhood
respiratory infections and impaired lung growth, and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency — an inherited
condition worth considering when COPD appears young, in a non-smoker, or with a strong family
history.

## Exacerbations

An exacerbation is an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond normal day-to-day variation,
usually triggered by a viral or bacterial infection. They matter more than the label suggests: each
one tends to worsen quality of life, accelerate lung-function decline, and predict the next, and a
history of them is the single strongest predictor of future ones. Recovery to baseline can take
weeks, and a proportion of people never return fully to their previous level.

## How it is generally managed

Smoking cessation above all; inhaled bronchodilator therapy, with inhaled corticosteroids where
indicated by exacerbations or eosinophil count; pulmonary rehabilitation; vaccination; assessment
for long-term oxygen therapy where resting oxygen levels are severely low; and a written plan for
what to do at an exacerbation. Nutrition, coexisting anxiety and depression, and treatable
comorbidities such as heart disease all affect symptoms and are managed alongside. The COPD
guideline note in this corpus covers therapy selection and review intervals.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Worsening breathlessness that does not respond to the usual reliever, breathlessness at rest,
confusion or unusual drowsiness, blue discoloration of the lips or fingers, chest pain, coughing
blood, or a fever with a marked change in sputum — these need urgent assessment rather than a
routine appointment. Drowsiness in someone struggling to breathe is a particularly serious sign,
because it can indicate rising carbon dioxide rather than tiredness. The emergency-symptom notes in
this corpus govern these situations.
