## Confirming the diagnosis

Asthma requires both a compatible symptom pattern and objective evidence of variable expiratory
airflow limitation. The symptom pattern is variable wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and
cough, worse at night or early morning and provoked by exercise, allergens, cold air or viral
infection. The objective test is spirometry before and after a bronchodilator, with reversibility
supporting the diagnosis.

A normal spirometry result when the person is well does not exclude asthma — variability is the
point, and a measurement taken on a good day measures the good day. Repeat testing while
symptomatic, peak-flow variability over two weeks, or bronchial challenge testing are the usual next
steps where the diagnosis remains uncertain. Confirming it before committing someone to long-term
inhaled therapy matters, because a substantial share of people carrying an asthma label do not have
objective confirmation of it.

## The central change in modern guidance

Treating asthma with a short-acting bronchodilator alone is no longer appropriate at any severity.
Every adult and adolescent with asthma receives inhaled-corticosteroid-containing therapy, because
reliever-only treatment leaves the underlying inflammation untreated and is associated with severe
exacerbations even in people whose symptoms are infrequent. Heavy reliever use — several canisters a
year — is a marker of risk in its own right.

## The stepwise structure

Treatment is arranged in steps, with the step chosen from current symptom control and exacerbation
history and reviewed rather than fixed:

- **Lower steps** — as-needed inhaled corticosteroid combined with a fast-onset long-acting
  bronchodilator, or regular low-dose inhaled corticosteroid with an as-needed reliever.
- **Middle steps** — regular low-dose inhaled corticosteroid with a long-acting bronchodilator, with
  the same combination inhaler used as the reliever in one of the described regimens.
- **Higher steps** — medium- or high-dose combination therapy, the addition of a long-acting
  muscarinic antagonist, then referral for severe-asthma assessment including biologic agents
  targeted to inflammatory phenotype.

Stepping down is considered after three or more months of good control. Before stepping up, inhaler
technique and adherence are checked, since between them they account for a large share of apparent
treatment failure, and a step-up that compensates for poor technique treats the wrong problem.

## Where guidance differs

The preferred reliever in mild asthma is a genuine point of divergence. Global strategy guidance
describes an inhaled-corticosteroid-and-formoterol combination as the preferred reliever across
steps; some national panel guidance describes a short-acting bronchodilator reliever alongside a
regular controller as remaining appropriate in mild disease. Answers should name which position they are describing.

## Review interval

Review one to three months after starting or changing therapy, then every three to twelve months,
and within one week after an exacerbation — the period when the reason for the exacerbation is still
recoverable.
