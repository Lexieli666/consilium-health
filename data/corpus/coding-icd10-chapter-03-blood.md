## The range and what it holds

Chapter 3 of ICD-10-CM covers D50 through D89: diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs, and
certain disorders involving the immune mechanism. It is the smaller half of the letter D. The first
part of that letter, D00 through D49, belongs to the neoplasm chapter and covers in-situ and benign
tumors, so a D code is not by itself a blood code and the number has to be read before the chapter
is decided.

## The blocks, in order

Nutritional anemias open the chapter at D50 to D53, with iron-deficiency anemia (anaemia) at D50
first, then the deficiencies of vitamin B12 and folate, then other dietary causes. Hemolytic anemias follow across D55 to D59,
divided into the inherited enzyme and hemoglobin disorders and the acquired forms.

Aplastic and other bone-marrow failure anemias occupy D60 to D64, and that block also holds the
residual category for anemias attributed to another disease, which is where anemia of chronic
disease and the anemia accompanying kidney failure are classified.

Coagulation defects, purpura and other hemorrhagic conditions run from D65 to D69, covering the
inherited factor deficiencies, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and the platelet disorders.
Other diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs follow at D70 to D77 — the white-cell
disorders, spleen disorders, and methemoglobinemia among them — with a short block at D78 for
complications of a procedure on the spleen.

The chapter closes at D80 to D89 with certain disorders involving the immune mechanism: the
immunodeficiencies, sarcoidosis, and the residual immune categories.

## What the chapter does not hold, and where those codes live

Cancers of the blood and lymphatic tissue are neoplasms, not blood diseases: leukemia, lymphoma and
myeloma are all classified in Chapter 2, and this chapter's anemia categories are used alongside
them rather than instead of them. Anemia complicating pregnancy, childbirth or the period after
delivery is classified in the obstetric chapter. Hemorrhagic disease of the newborn belongs to the
perinatal chapter.

The boundary that matters most in practice is with abnormal findings. A low hemoglobin (haemoglobin)
or hematocrit on a laboratory report, with no diagnosis of anemia recorded, is an abnormal blood
finding and is classified in Chapter 18, not here. Enlarged lymph nodes with no cause established
are likewise a sign rather than a disease. These codes move into Chapter 3 only when a diagnosis is
documented.

## Codes from other chapters that blood questions commonly need

Anemia is frequently a manifestation rather than a diagnosis in its own right, so the categories
here carry sequencing instructions that reach into other chapters: chronic kidney disease from the
genitourinary chapter, an underlying neoplasm from Chapter 2, and the adverse effect or poisoning
codes from Chapter 19 when a drug is responsible. Chronic blood loss as the cause of iron deficiency
is itself coded from whichever chapter owns the bleeding site. Long-term drug therapy, dietary
counseling encounters and family history all come from the factors-influencing-health chapter.
