## What it is

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot fill with or eject blood well enough
to meet the body's needs at normal filling pressures. The name is misleading in a way worth
correcting: it does not mean the heart has stopped or is about to. It is a chronic condition that is
managed, often for many years.

It is classified by ejection fraction — the proportion of blood in the left ventricle expelled with
each beat — because the evidence for treatment differs sharply between the groups: reduced (40
percent or below), mildly reduced (41 to 49 percent), and preserved (50 percent or above). Preserved
ejection fraction accounts for roughly half of cases and is not a milder form; it is a different
problem, one of stiffness and impaired filling rather than weak contraction.

## How it is usually recognized

Breathlessness on exertion, then at lower levels of exertion, then when lying flat; waking at night
breathless; swelling of the ankles and legs; fatigue; reduced exercise tolerance; and weight gain
over days that reflects retained fluid rather than tissue. Distinguishing fluid weight from other
weight change matters, because a rise of two or three kilograms across a few days is a fluid signal
and a reason to act.

Confirming the diagnosis involves natriuretic peptide blood testing and echocardiography, which
establishes the ejection fraction that determines the treatment pathway.

## What contributes to it

Coronary artery disease and previous myocardial infarction are the most common causes, followed by
long-standing high blood pressure. Also: valve disease, atrial fibrillation, cardiomyopathies
including genetic and post-viral forms, alcohol, some chemotherapy agents, thyroid disease, and
iron overload. Diabetes, obesity, chronic kidney disease and sleep apnea (apnoea) commonly coexist
and worsen the course.

## Why it is treated

Treatment reduces symptoms, hospital admissions and mortality. Modern therapy for reduced ejection
fraction has changed the outlook substantially, and a proportion of people recover ventricular
function on it — which is why the ejection fraction is reassessed after therapy is established
rather than treated as a fixed property. The four foundational classes and their titration are
covered by the heart-failure guideline note in this corpus.

## How it is generally managed

Disease-modifying drug therapy according to ejection fraction; diuretics for congestion; treating
the underlying cause and the coexisting conditions; devices in selected people; cardiac
rehabilitation; vaccination; and structured self-management — daily weights, recognizing early
fluid retention, and knowing when to seek help. Sodium and fluid advice is individualized rather
than uniform.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Breathlessness at rest or that wakes the person and does not settle when upright, chest pain, rapid
weight gain over a few days, fainting, a very fast or irregular pulse with symptoms, or confusion —
these need urgent assessment. Pink frothy sputum with severe breathlessness is an emergency. The
emergency-symptom notes in this corpus govern these situations.
