## What it is

Osteoarthritis is a disorder of the whole joint — cartilage, underlying bone, ligaments, joint
lining and surrounding muscle — in which cartilage is progressively lost and the bone underneath
remodels in response. It most often affects the knees, hips, hands and spine.

The description of it as "wear and tear" is inaccurate enough to be worth correcting, because it
leads people to rest the joint. The process is an active one involving inflammation and attempted
repair, not simple mechanical erosion, and the muscle weakness that follows from reduced use makes
the joint worse rather than protecting it. That is why exercise is the first-line treatment and not
merely an adjunct.

## How it is usually recognized

Joint pain that is worse with activity and better with rest, short-lived stiffness after inactivity
— characteristically under 30 minutes in the morning, which distinguishes it from inflammatory
arthritis — reduced range of movement, a grating sensation on movement, and bony swelling. Onset is
gradual, over months to years, and usually asymmetric.

The diagnosis is generally clinical in a person over 45 with activity-related pain and no prolonged
morning stiffness. Imaging is not required to make it, and the relationship between what an X-ray
shows and what a person feels is weak in both directions — substantial radiographic change with few
symptoms, and significant pain with modest change, are both common. Imaging that is done for
reassurance frequently achieves the opposite.

## What contributes to it

Age; previous joint injury or surgery, which is the dominant factor in a young person with knee
osteoarthritis; obesity, which acts both mechanically and metabolically; occupational and sporting
loading; joint shape and alignment; muscle weakness; genetic factors; and female sex, particularly
for hand and knee disease after menopause.

## Why it is treated

Pain and reduced function affect independence, sleep, mood and cardiovascular fitness, and the
inactivity that follows compounds all of them. Treatment aims to reduce pain and maintain function,
and the condition is manageable over long periods; it does not inevitably progress to joint
replacement.

## How it is generally managed

Structured exercise — combining muscle strengthening, range of movement and aerobic activity — is
the core treatment for every affected joint, together with weight management where relevant.
Education, pacing, appropriate footwear, walking aids where useful, and heat or cold for symptom
relief support it. Pharmacologic options are adjuncts rather than the foundation, with topical
agents preferred where the joint is accessible. Joint replacement is considered where pain and
functional limitation persist despite adequate non-surgical management. The osteoarthritis guideline
note in this corpus covers the sequence and the evidence behind it.

## When further assessment is advised

A hot, swollen, acutely painful joint with fever suggests infection or crystal arthritis and needs
urgent assessment — this is not an osteoarthritis flare. Prolonged morning stiffness, multiple
symmetric small-joint involvement, or systemic symptoms suggest inflammatory arthritis instead.
Sudden joint locking or giving way, or new neurological symptoms with spinal pain, also warrant
prompt assessment.
