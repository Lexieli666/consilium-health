## What it is

Coronary artery disease is the build-up of atherosclerotic plaque in the arteries supplying the
heart muscle. Plaque develops over decades, beginning with injury to the artery lining and the
accumulation of cholesterol-rich material beneath it. Two distinct mechanisms produce symptoms, and
confusing them is the source of most misunderstanding about the condition:

- **Gradual narrowing** limits blood flow when demand rises, producing predictable exertional chest
  discomfort — stable angina.
- **Plaque rupture** exposes material that triggers clot formation, which can occlude the artery
  abruptly. This produces an acute coronary syndrome, and it can happen at a plaque that was never
  narrow enough to cause symptoms. That is why a normal stress test in the past does not mean a
  heart attack cannot happen now.

## How it is usually recognized

Stable angina is classically a pressure, tightness or heaviness in the chest, brought on by exertion
or emotional stress, relieved within minutes by rest, and sometimes spreading to the arm, neck, jaw
or back. It is a reproducible pattern — the same amount of effort brings it on.

Presentations are frequently not classical. Breathlessness on exertion without chest discomfort,
unusual fatigue, nausea, or discomfort felt mainly in the jaw, back or upper abdomen are all
recognized presentations, and are relatively more common in women, older people, and people with
diabetes, in whom nerve damage can blunt the pain. The consequence is delayed presentation, and the
delay is what causes the harm.

## What contributes to it

Smoking, high blood pressure, raised LDL cholesterol, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, a diet
high in saturated fat and low in vegetables and fiber, chronic kidney disease, inflammatory
conditions, and a family history of premature coronary disease. Age and male sex raise baseline risk
and cannot be modified; the rest of the list can be, which is what makes the risk estimate
actionable rather than merely descriptive.

## Why it is treated

Untreated coronary disease leads to myocardial infarction, heart failure from damaged muscle,
arrhythmia, and death. Secondary prevention after a diagnosis substantially reduces the rate of
further events, and the effect comes from the package rather than from any single element — covered
by the secondary-prevention guideline note in this corpus.

## How it is generally managed

Risk-factor treatment, antiplatelet and lipid-lowering therapy, agents to control angina symptoms,
cardiac rehabilitation, and revascularization — stenting or bypass surgery — where symptoms persist
despite medical therapy or where the pattern of disease indicates it. Revascularization relieves
symptoms reliably; it does not remove the need for the medical therapy underneath.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Chest discomfort at rest, discomfort lasting more than a few minutes, discomfort with breathlessness,
sweating, nausea or light-headedness, or angina that is newly more frequent or more easily provoked
— these are emergencies and need immediate medical attention rather than a routine appointment.
Waiting to see whether it settles is the most common and most costly error. The emergency-symptom
notes in this corpus govern these situations.
