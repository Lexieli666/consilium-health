> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## The diagnosis is spirometric

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is not diagnosed from symptoms alone. A post-bronchodilator
ratio of forced expiratory volume in one second to forced vital capacity below 0.70 confirms
persistent airflow limitation, in a person with compatible symptoms — breathlessness on exertion,
chronic cough, sputum production — and a relevant exposure history, most often tobacco smoke, and
also biomass fuel and occupational dust or fumes. The degree of airflow limitation is graded
separately, using the forced expiratory volume as a percentage of predicted, and correlates only
loosely with how the person actually feels.

## Assessment combines symptoms and exacerbations

Two axes are assessed rather than severity alone. Symptom burden is measured with a standard
questionnaire or breathlessness scale, and exacerbation history over the past year is recorded, with
particular weight on any exacerbation requiring hospital admission. Together they place the person
in a group that determines initial therapy. The exacerbation axis, not the symptom axis, identifies
the group at highest risk — and the best predictor of a future exacerbation is a past one.

## Initial pharmacologic therapy

Guidance describes initial therapy by group rather than by severity of airflow limitation:

- **Low symptom burden, no significant exacerbation history** — a long-acting bronchodilator.
- **High symptom burden, no significant exacerbation history** — dual long-acting bronchodilator
  therapy, combining a muscarinic antagonist and a beta-agonist.
- **Significant exacerbation history** — dual long-acting bronchodilator therapy, with an inhaled
  corticosteroid added where the blood eosinophil count is high or where features of coexisting
  asthma are present.

The blood eosinophil count is the marker used to predict who benefits from an inhaled
corticosteroid. The decision rests on a marker rather than being applied to everyone because
inhaled corticosteroid use in this population carries an increased risk of pneumonia, so the benefit
has to be identifiable to be worth the harm.

## The measures with the largest effect are not inhalers

Smoking cessation is the only intervention shown to alter the rate of decline in lung function.
Pulmonary rehabilitation improves breathlessness and exercise capacity more than any inhaler does.
Vaccination against influenza, pneumococcal disease, COVID-19 and, where recommended, RSV reduces
exacerbations. Long-term oxygen therapy is assessed for in people with severe resting hypoxemia,
where it affects survival — and is not indicated for breathlessness in the absence of that
hypoxemia, which is a frequent misunderstanding.

## Review interval

Review at least annually, after every exacerbation, and after any change in inhaled therapy, with
inhaler technique checked at each review. Escalation without a technique check is one of the more
common avoidable errors in the pathway.
