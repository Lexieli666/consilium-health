## What it is

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways in which the airway lining is inflamed and
the surrounding muscle is prone to constrict, producing airflow obstruction that varies over time
and is at least partly reversible. The variability is the defining feature: lung function and
symptoms change from day to day and within a day. That distinguishes it from the persistent
obstruction of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, although the two can coexist, and the
combination is more common in older people with a smoking history.

## How it is usually recognized

The characteristic pattern is wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough that vary in
intensity, are often worse at night or in the early morning, and are provoked by identifiable
triggers. Cough alone, particularly at night, is a recognized presentation and is often attributed
to something else for a long time before the pattern is noticed. Symptoms that are entirely constant,
or that never respond to a bronchodilator, prompt consideration of an alternative explanation.

Objective evidence of variable airflow limitation is required as well as the symptom pattern; the
asthma guideline note in this corpus covers it.

## Common triggers

Viral respiratory infections, house dust mite, pollen, animal dander, mold, tobacco smoke, air
pollution, cold air, exercise, strong odors, occupational exposures, and certain medications —
including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and beta-blockers in susceptible people. Emotional
stress and laughter can precipitate symptoms, which is not the same as the condition being
psychological in origin. Identifying an individual's triggers is part of management, since avoidance
where practical reduces the treatment burden; occupational asthma is worth identifying early,
because it can remit if the exposure ends soon enough.

## Control and severity are different things

This distinction matters and is frequently confused. **Control** describes how much the condition is
currently affecting the person — daytime symptoms, night waking, reliever use, and activity
limitation — and can change within weeks. **Severity** describes how much treatment is required to
achieve control, and is assessed retrospectively once treatment is established. Poor control is
usually a treatment, adherence or inhaler-technique problem rather than evidence of severe disease,
and treating it as severity leads to escalation where a technique check was needed.

## How it is generally managed

Regular anti-inflammatory inhaled therapy, a reliever, trigger avoidance where practical, inhaler
technique training, a written action plan describing what to do when symptoms worsen, and treatment
of contributing conditions such as allergic rhinitis, reflux and obesity. Stepwise treatment
structure and review intervals are covered by the asthma guideline note in this corpus.

## When urgent assessment is advised

An attack that does not respond to reliever treatment, or that returns within a few hours, requires
prompt medical assessment. Immediate emergency attention is advised for difficulty speaking in full
sentences, breathlessness at rest, drowsiness or confusion, blue discoloration of the lips or
fingers, or a chest that has become quiet because too little air is moving to produce a wheeze — the
absence of wheeze during a severe attack is a serious sign, not a reassuring one. The
emergency-symptom notes in this corpus govern these situations.
