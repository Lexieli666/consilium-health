> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## Two different effects, on two different timescales

Physical activity affects anxiety in two ways that are worth separating, because people who expect
one and get the other conclude it has not worked.

The **acute** effect follows a single session and lasts hours: reduced tension and lowered
physiological arousal after moderate activity. It is reliable enough to be used deliberately, as
something done on a bad afternoon rather than only as a long-term investment.

The **sustained** effect builds over weeks of regular activity and shows up as a lower baseline
level of worry and tension. It accumulates slowly and is easy to miss while it is happening, which
is why a record kept over weeks is more informative than how any single day felt.

The anxiety guideline note in this corpus covers where first-line treatment sits. Activity is
described as a useful adjunct alongside it, not as a replacement for it, and someone whose symptoms
are severe or worsening needs the treatment question answered rather than a longer walk.

## What kind, and how much

Moderate aerobic activity accumulated across the week is the pattern with the most supporting
evidence — brisk walking, cycling, swimming, anything sustained enough to raise breathing while
still allowing conversation. Resistance training has a smaller but real supporting literature.
Activity done outdoors, or with other people, tends to be sustained longer, and the amount that is
actually done matters more than the theoretically optimal amount that is not.

Starting small is not a compromise here. A short daily walk that continues for three months
outperforms an ambitious program abandoned in week two, and the abandonment itself tends to feed the
self-critical thinking that the activity was meant to address.

## The part specific to anxiety: bodily sensations

Exercise raises heart rate, breathing rate and sweating — the same sensations that anxiety produces
and that people who are anxious about their health often interpret as a sign something is wrong.
This is why some people avoid exertion, and why exercise sometimes feels worse before it feels
better.

Encountering those sensations in a setting where the cause is obvious is part of how the avoidance
loosens. It is worth knowing in advance that the sensations will occur and are expected, so a raised
heart rate during a brisk walk reads as the walk rather than as evidence.

Where avoidance is entrenched, or where chest symptoms or fainting are part of the picture, this is
a conversation for a clinician before starting rather than something to work through alone.

## Timing, caffeine and what not to expect

Activity late in the evening keeps some people alert at bedtime and suits others; it is worth
testing rather than assuming. Caffeine taken to get through a workout produces the same
physiological arousal that the activity is meant to reduce, and high intakes are worth reviewing
alongside any activity plan. Activity does not remove worry, and expecting it to is the most common
route to concluding it failed.
