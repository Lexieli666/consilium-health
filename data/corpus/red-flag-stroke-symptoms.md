> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## Call emergency services immediately

Any sudden onset of the following is treated as a stroke until proven otherwise:

- **Face** — drooping on one side, or an uneven smile.
- **Arm** — weakness or numbness in one arm, or one arm drifting downward when both are raised.
- **Speech** — slurred speech, difficulty finding words, or difficulty understanding others.
- **Time** — note the time symptoms began, or the last time the person was known to be well, and
  call emergency services now.

Symptoms beyond the four above that carry the same urgency: sudden numbness or weakness of the leg
or of one whole side; sudden confusion; sudden loss or blurring of vision in one or both eyes;
sudden difficulty walking, dizziness, or loss of balance and coordination; and sudden severe
headache with no known cause.

## Why the time of onset matters so much

Treatments that restore blood flow to the brain — clot-dissolving drugs and mechanical clot removal
— are effective only within a limited window from symptom onset, and their benefit falls steeply
with every passing minute. The single most useful piece of information a caller can provide is when
the person was last known to be well. Where symptoms were present on waking, the relevant time is
when the person was last seen normal, not when they woke.

Brain tissue is lost continuously while an artery is blocked. This is the reason emergency services
are called rather than waiting for a routine appointment or driving to a clinic, and the reason a
stroke is treated as time-critical in the same way as a heart attack.

## Symptoms that resolve are still an emergency

Symptoms that disappear within minutes may indicate a transient ischemic attack. The event itself
has ended, but the risk of a full stroke in the following days is substantially raised, and it is
highest in the first 48 hours. Resolution is not reassurance and does not convert this into a
routine appointment — urgent assessment is what reduces the subsequent risk.

## What not to do

Do not give food, drink, or medication, including aspirin, to someone with suspected stroke:
swallowing may be impaired, and the type of stroke — clot or bleed — determines the treatment and
cannot be established without imaging. Do not wait to see whether symptoms improve. Do not let the
person drive.

## While waiting

Keep the person resting, comfortable and observed, note the time of onset and what was observed, and
gather a list of their medications if it can be done without leaving them unattended. Anticoagulant
use is particularly important for the receiving team to know.
