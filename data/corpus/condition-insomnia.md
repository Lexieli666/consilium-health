## What it is

Insomnia is difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking earlier than intended, despite
adequate opportunity to sleep, together with daytime consequences. Both halves are required. Sleeping
six hours and functioning well is not insomnia; sleeping six hours and being impaired by it may be.
The condition is defined by the mismatch between sleep obtained and sleep needed, not by a clock
value.

Short-term insomnia lasting days to weeks, usually with an identifiable precipitant, is common and
often self-limiting. Chronic insomnia disorder — at least three nights per week for at least three
months — is a distinct condition that persists after the original precipitant has resolved.

## Why it persists after the trigger goes

This is the part that explains the treatment. An episode is typically precipitated by stress,
illness or a schedule change. What perpetuates it are the responses: going to bed earlier to
"catch up", staying in bed awake, daytime napping, and increasing effort to fall asleep. Time in bed
rises while sleep does not, so the bed becomes associated with wakefulness, and the effort itself
increases arousal. The condition then maintains itself independently of what started it — which is
why treatment targets those perpetuating behaviors rather than the original stressor.

## How it is usually recognized

The account of the sleep pattern, supported by a sleep diary kept over one to two weeks. Diaries
matter because recall systematically overestimates how long it took to fall asleep and underestimates
total sleep — the periods of sleep between awakenings are not remembered.

## What else presents as insomnia

Obstructive sleep apnea (apnoea), restless legs syndrome, circadian rhythm disorders including shift
work and delayed sleep phase, chronic pain, depression, anxiety, and the effects of caffeine,
alcohol, nicotine, stimulants and some prescribed medications. Screening for these is part of the
assessment rather than a step taken only when treatment fails, because treating the wrong condition
is the most common reason for non-response. Alcohol deserves specific mention: it shortens the time
to fall asleep and fragments the second half of the night, so it is frequently reported as a remedy
by someone whose sleep it is degrading.

## Why it is treated

Chronic insomnia is associated with impaired daytime function, reduced quality of life, and
increased risk of depression, and it complicates the management of coexisting physical illness. It
also responds durably to non-drug treatment, which makes long-term reliance on hypnotics avoidable
for most people.

## How it is generally managed

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is first-line, with medication second-line and
time-limited. The components, the evidence, and the specific point that sleep hygiene advice alone
is not effective treatment are covered by the insomnia guideline note in this corpus.

## When further assessment is advised

Loud snoring with witnessed pauses in breathing, excessive daytime sleepiness, falling asleep while
driving, acting out dreams, or sleep disturbance alongside low mood and hopelessness all warrant
assessment beyond insomnia treatment.
