> **Educational summary — not medical advice.** This note is reference material for a software
> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## There is no single diabetes diet

This is the clearest statement in current nutrition guidance and the one most at odds with popular
expectation. Several eating patterns — Mediterranean-style, low-carbohydrate, vegetarian and vegan,
and the dietary pattern used for blood pressure — have evidence supporting them, and no single one
is established as superior for everyone. The pattern a person will actually maintain, adapted to
their preferences, culture, budget and cooking situation, is described as the deciding factor.

What the effective patterns share: more non-starchy vegetables, whole rather than refined grains,
legumes, nuts, and minimally processed foods; fewer refined grains, added sugars, sugar-sweetened
drinks and highly processed foods.

## Carbohydrate

Carbohydrate has the largest single effect on blood glucose after a meal, so quantity and type both
matter. Guidance describes reducing refined carbohydrate and sugar-sweetened beverages as the
highest-yield change. Carbohydrate counting is one approach for people using insulin; simpler
approaches — consistent portions, the plate method, choosing whole over refined — are described as
adequate for many people not on insulin.

Low-carbohydrate patterns have evidence for improving glucose control and are an accepted option.
They require review of glucose-lowering medication first, because reducing carbohydrate while
continuing insulin or an agent that stimulates insulin release can cause low blood sugar. That
review is a matter for the person's own clinician, and is the reason this system does not recommend
a carbohydrate target.

## Weight

Where body weight is above the healthy range, weight reduction improves glucose control, blood
pressure and lipids together. Sustained reduction of five percent produces measurable benefit, with
more at greater reductions, and substantial reduction can put type 2 diabetes into remission in some
people — most likely earlier in the course of the condition. Remission means normal glucose without
glucose-lowering medication; it is not a cure, and monitoring continues.

## Practical points that come up repeatedly

- **"Sugar-free" is not "carbohydrate-free."** Starch raises glucose as effectively as sugar.
- **Fruit is not off-limits.** Whole fruit is part of every recommended pattern; fruit juice behaves
  more like a sugar-sweetened drink.
- **Alcohol** can cause delayed low blood sugar in people on insulin or insulin-stimulating agents,
  particularly when drunk without food.
- **Meal timing** matters most for people on medication that acts at fixed times.
- **Non-nutritive sweeteners** are described as a reasonable substitute for sugar-sweetened drinks
  where they displace them, rather than as a benefit in themselves.

Referral to a registered dietitian for individualized medical nutrition therapy is described as part
of standard diabetes care rather than an optional extra. Targets, monitoring intervals and drug
classes are covered by the diabetes guideline notes in this corpus.
