> project. It does not diagnose or treat, and it must not be used for real medical decisions.

## The dietary pattern with the most evidence

The dietary approach most consistently described for blood pressure emphasizes vegetables, fruit,
whole grains, low-fat dairy, poultry, fish, beans, nuts and vegetable oils, while limiting saturated
fat, full-fat dairy, fatty meat, sweets and sugar-sweetened drinks. It is a whole pattern rather
than a single food, and the trials that established it changed everything on the plate at once —
which is why substituting one "blood pressure food" into an otherwise unchanged diet does not
reproduce the result.

The reduction seen with the pattern alone is meaningful, and combining it with sodium reduction
produces a larger effect than either alone.

## Sodium

Sodium reduction is the single most-studied dietary change for blood pressure. Commonly stated
targets are under 2,300 mg per day, with a further benefit described at 1,500 mg for many adults.
Any reduction helps: the relationship is graded, so moving from very high intake to merely high
intake is worthwhile even if a target is not reached.

The practical point that surprises people: most dietary sodium does not come from the salt shaker.
The large majority is already in packaged and restaurant food — bread, processed meat, cheese,
sauces, soups, snacks and prepared meals. Cooking at home from unprocessed ingredients does more
than removing salt from the table, and reading labels for sodium per serving is more effective than
any single substitution.

Salt substitutes containing potassium chloride are described as reducing blood pressure, but they
are not appropriate for everyone — particularly people with chronic kidney disease or on medication
that raises potassium — and are a matter for the person's own clinician.

## Potassium

Higher dietary potassium is associated with lower blood pressure, and increasing it through food —
vegetables, fruit, beans, dairy — is the described route rather than supplements. The same caution
about kidney disease and potassium-raising medication applies.

## Alcohol

Alcohol raises blood pressure in a dose-related way, and reducing intake lowers it. Commonly stated
limits are up to two drinks per day for men and one for women, with less being better. For someone
drinking well above that, reduction is one of the more reliable non-drug interventions available.

## Weight

Weight reduction lowers blood pressure roughly in proportion to the amount lost, and the effect
begins early rather than requiring a target weight to be reached. A modest, sustained reduction is
described as worthwhile in its own right.

## What is not established

Specific "superfoods", vinegar, garlic supplements and similar single-ingredient remedies do not
have evidence comparable to the whole-pattern approach. Caffeine raises blood pressure acutely but
habitual intake has little sustained effect in most people. The hypertension guideline notes in this
corpus cover thresholds, targets and when drug treatment is added.
