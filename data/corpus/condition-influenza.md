## What it is

Influenza is a respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses, of which types A and B cause
seasonal epidemics. Influenza A is subtyped by two surface proteins, hemagglutinin and
neuraminidase — the H and N in designations such as H1N1 and H3N2. The virus changes continuously
through small mutations, which is why immunity from one season is incomplete against the next and
why the vaccine is reformulated annually rather than given once.

It is more severe than a common cold in both symptoms and consequences, and it causes substantial
seasonal hospitalization and death, concentrated in older adults, young children, pregnant people
and those with chronic conditions.

## How it is usually recognized

Abrupt onset — people can often name the hour — with fever, chills, muscle aches, headache, marked
fatigue and dry cough. Sore throat and nasal symptoms occur but are less prominent than in a cold.
Fever typically lasts three to five days; cough and fatigue commonly persist two weeks or more.
Gastrointestinal symptoms occur more often in children than adults.

Laboratory confirmation is not required to manage a typical case during a known outbreak, and is
used where the result would change management — in hospital, in high-risk people, or where an
antiviral decision or outbreak control depends on it.

## Complications

Pneumonia is the main one, either from the virus itself or from a secondary bacterial infection.
Secondary bacterial pneumonia classically appears as a deterioration after several days of
improvement, which is a pattern worth recognizing. Other complications include worsening of
underlying asthma, COPD, heart failure or diabetes, and less commonly inflammation of the heart
muscle, brain or muscle tissue. Influenza is also associated with a transient increase in the risk
of myocardial infarction and stroke in the weeks after infection.

## How it is generally managed

Supportive care for most otherwise healthy people: rest, fluids, and symptomatic relief. Antiviral
treatment is targeted rather than universal, and is most effective when started early — the timing
threshold and the groups in whom treatment is recommended are covered by the influenza guideline
note in this corpus. Antibiotics have no effect on the virus and are reserved for a bacterial
complication.

Annual vaccination is the principal preventive measure, alongside staying home while febrile, hand
hygiene and respiratory etiquette.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Difficulty breathing or breathlessness at rest, chest pain, persistent dizziness or confusion,
inability to keep fluids down, seizures, severe muscle pain or weakness, or symptoms that improve
and then return with fever and worse cough — these need urgent assessment. In children, additional
signs include fast or labored breathing, blue lips, no tears when crying, and significantly reduced
wet diapers. The emergency-symptom notes in this corpus govern these situations.
