## What it is

An upper respiratory infection is a viral infection of the nose, sinuses, throat and upper airway.
Rhinoviruses cause the largest share; coronaviruses, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza,
adenovirus and others account for the rest. It is self-limiting in the great majority of otherwise
healthy people.

The distinction from influenza is clinically useful. A cold begins gradually, is dominated by nasal
symptoms and sore throat, and rarely produces high fever or prostration in adults. Influenza
typically begins abruptly with fever, muscle aches and marked malaise, with respiratory symptoms
following. The distinction is not reliable enough to be certain in an individual, but it usefully
shifts the probability.

## How it is usually recognized

Nasal congestion and discharge, sneezing, sore throat, cough, mild headache and malaise, sometimes a
low-grade fever. Symptoms typically peak at two to three days and resolve over seven to ten days.
Cough frequently outlasts everything else, persisting two to three weeks, which is normal and not by
itself evidence of a bacterial infection or of treatment failure.

Discharge changing from clear to yellow or green is part of the normal course, reflecting immune
cells rather than bacterial infection. This is the single most common reason people seek antibiotics
for a cold, and it is not an indication for them.

## What contributes to it

Exposure to infected people, which is why incidence rises with young children in the household and
in the colder months when people are indoors. Transmission is by respiratory droplets and by
contaminated hands and surfaces reaching the eyes or nose. Hand hygiene reduces transmission
measurably; the belief that cold exposure itself causes colds does not survive examination.

## How it is generally managed

Supportive care: rest, fluids, and symptomatic relief. Antibiotics have no effect on viral infection
and carry real harms — adverse effects, disruption of normal flora, and antimicrobial resistance —
which is why antibiotic stewardship for these presentations is an explicit guideline topic rather
than a matter of preference. Over-the-counter symptomatic treatments have modest effects on symptoms
and none on duration, and several are not appropriate for young children.

## When further assessment is advised

Symptoms worsening after an initial improvement, fever returning after it had settled, symptoms
persisting beyond ten days without improvement, severe facial pain or one-sided facial swelling,
severe sore throat with difficulty swallowing or drooling, or breathlessness — these suggest a
complication such as bacterial sinusitis, pneumonia, or a throat infection needing assessment.

People at higher risk of complications — infants, older adults, pregnancy, and anyone with chronic
lung, heart, kidney or liver disease, diabetes, or a weakened immune system — warrant earlier
assessment than an otherwise healthy adult.

## When urgent assessment is advised

Difficulty breathing, chest pain, a stiff neck with severe headache, confusion, persistent vomiting
preventing fluid intake, or signs of dehydration require urgent assessment. The emergency-symptom
notes in this corpus govern these situations.
